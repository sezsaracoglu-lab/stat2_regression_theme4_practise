🎯 Explanatory and response
“The explanatory variable is…”, or which goes on the x-axis
Naming the roles
The explanatory variable does the explaining and goes on x. Ask which one you would use to predict the other. Correlation has no roles; regression does.
🔗 What r means
Two values of r, and which is “stronger”
Strength is the size, direction is the sign
Compare |r|. −0.8 and +0.8 are equally strong. Only 0 is weak.
“Positively, negatively or not associated?”
The direction of the relationship
Ask what happens to y as x rises. Up together → positive. One up, other down → negative.
A question about r and the slope together
They always share a sign
b₁ = r × s(y) ⁄ s(x), and both standard deviations are positive, so the sign of the slope is the sign of r. Always.
Data averaged into groups, then correlated
Aggregation inflates r
Averaging removes the scatter within each group and leaves only the between-group pattern, so r goes up. The underlying relationship has not changed.
📈 Reading a scatterplot
A scatterplot and “the correlation is about…”
Estimating r by eye
Direction from the slope of the cloud, strength from how tight it is. A tight rising band is about +0.9; a vague one about +0.5; a shapeless blob about 0.
A plot with two groups and a claim about both
Compare the two trends, nothing more
You may compare the steepness and the level you can SEE. You may not run either trend past the end of its data.
A curved cloud and a question about r
r only measures straight-line strength
A strong curve can give r near 0. Always look at the plot before trusting r.
🧮 Computing r and the line
Raw x and y values and “compute r”
Pearson’s r by hand
Deviation columns first (they must each sum to 0), then SP = Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ), SS(x), SS(y), then r = SP ⁄ √(SS(x)·SS(y)).
“Find the least-squares line”
Slope then intercept, in that order
b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x). Then b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄. Check: the line must pass through (x̄, ȳ).
Only two data points
r is always ±1
Two points define a line exactly. The sign follows the direction; the size is always 1. Nothing about strength is being measured.
📏 Reading slope and intercept
“What does the slope mean?”
Change in predicted y per one unit of x
Say it with units and with “on average”. It is a statement about predicted averages, never a promise about one individual.
“What does the intercept mean?”
Predicted y when x = 0
Meaningful only if x = 0 is inside the data. Often it is not, and then it is just where the line happens to cross.
🔮 Prediction and extrapolation
“Predict y when x = …”
Substitute into the line
ŷ = b₀ + b₁x. One substitution. Then ask whether that x is inside the observed range.
A prediction far outside the data range
Extrapolation
The arithmetic still works and the answer is still worthless. If the x is well outside the data, the honest answer is that you cannot say.
📉 Residuals
“What is the residual?”
Observed minus predicted
Predict first, then subtract: residual = y − ŷ. Positive means the point sits above the line. Mind the order — ŷ − y gets the sign backwards.
A residual plot to judge
Shape is the message
Random scatter around zero → the line fits. A curve → the relationship is not straight. A fan → the spread changes with x.
🥧 R² — how much is explained
“What percentage of the variation is explained?”
r², as a percentage
Square r and read it as a percentage. r = 0.87 gives 76%, not 87%.
“Which measures only the strength?”
r² has no direction
r carries strength and direction; the slope is a rate with units; r² is pure strength, because squaring destroyed the sign.
⚠️ Outliers and influence
A point far out along x
High leverage, probably influential
A point far from x̄ has a long lever on the slope. Ask which way it pulls the line, then say what removing it would do.
A point with a big residual but an ordinary x
An outlier in y, usually not influential
It sits off the line but near the middle of x, so it has little leverage. It inflates the scatter without moving the slope much.
🕵️ Causation and lurking variables
An association and a tempting causal story
Association is not causation
Name the third variable. Three different stories — direct cause, reverse cause, common response — produce identical scatterplots.
Two explanations tangled together
Confounding
Their effects cannot be separated by these data. Say which two are confounded and what design would separate them.
“How do you establish cause and effect?”
A well-designed experiment
Random assignment is the only thing that breaks the link with lurking variables. No amount of correlation does it.
🖥️ Reading computer output
A table of Coef, SE Coef, T and P
Find the slope row first
The predictor’s Coef is the slope; the Constant’s Coef is the intercept. Read the slope in the study’s own units.
A big R² and a question about whether the model is good
R² says nothing about shape
A high R² can sit on a curve. You need the scatterplot or the residual plot before you call a regression useful.

Question 1

🎯 Explanatory and response

Naming the explanatory variable

I want to examine the relationship between gas mileage of cars and the engine size (displacement in cubic inches). The explanatory variable is:
  • aengine size.
  • bgas mileage.
  • cI could use either one.
🔎 What it is really asking

Which of the two variables is doing the explaining — and therefore belongs on the x-axis?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Naming the roles. No arithmetic; the whole question is which variable explains which.

🛠️ The procedure

Ask which direction the explanation runs: would you use A to predict B, or B to predict A? The one doing the predicting is the explanatory variable and goes on x.

🧮 The working
Which way round does the story make sense?

Engine size explains gas mileage. A bigger engine burns more fuel, so mileage drops. That is a mechanism.

Gas mileage explaining engine size is not a mechanism. A car’s mileage does not determine how big its engine is — the engine was built first.

So engine size is explanatory (x) and gas mileage is the response (y).

Worth keeping straight: correlation has no roles — r(x, y) = r(y, x), so for correlation alone the question would not arise. Regression does, because the line predicts y from x, and regressing y on x gives a different line from regressing x on y.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.Gas mileage is the response — the thing being explained. It goes on y.
c.For correlation, either order would do. But this question is about the explanatory variable, which only exists once you have decided which variable predicts which. Engine size is the only one with a mechanism behind it.
🏷️ Explanatory = explains = x-axis. Ask “which one would I use to predict the other?”

Question 2

🔗 What r means

Strength versus direction

A recent article in an educational research journal reports a correlation of +0.8 between math achievement and overall math aptitude. It also reports a correlation of –0.8 between math achievement and a math anxiety test. Which of the following interpretations is the most correct?
  • aThe correlation of +0.8 indicates a stronger relationship than the correlation of –0.8.
  • bThe correlation of +0.8 is just as strong as the correlation of –0.8.
  • cIt is impossible to tell which correlation is stronger.
🔎 What it is really asking

Is +0.8 a stronger relationship than −0.8, or the same strength?

🧭 Which type of question this is

The single most examined fact about r: strength and direction are two different things, carried by two different parts of the number.

🛠️ The procedure

Strength is the size (how far from 0). Direction is the sign. Compare |r|, and ignore the sign entirely when the question asks about strength.

🧮 The working
|+0.8| = 0.8 and |−0.8| = 0.8. Equally strong.

What the sign is telling you instead:

+0.8 — higher aptitude goes with higher achievement. They move together.
−0.8 — higher anxiety goes with lower achievement. They move opposite.

Both relationships are equally tight. One is just pointing downhill.

Think of it as a number line with 0 in the middle and ±1 at the ends. Distance from zero is strength; which side you are on is direction. −0.9 is stronger than +0.3, and it is not even close.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.This reads the sign as if it were part of the size. It is not — a minus sign says “downhill”, not “weaker”.
c.It is perfectly possible to tell: compare the absolute values. This option would only be right if one of the correlations were missing.
🏷️ Size = strength, sign = direction. Judge strength by |r| and nothing else.

Question 3

🔗 What r means

Reading the direction from the context

Are the following two variables positively associated, negatively associated or not associated?

Let X be how much gas you have in your tank (in gallons).
Let Y be the cost to fill your gas tank.
  • aThe two variables are positively associated.
  • bThe two variables are negatively associated.
  • cThe two variables are not associated.
🔎 What it is really asking

As the gas already in your tank goes up, does the cost of filling it go up or down?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Direction from the situation. Read it as a small story before reaching for any rule.

🛠️ The procedure

Pick two concrete cases — one with a low x and one with a high x — and see what happens to y.

🧮 The working
Nearly empty tank (x small): you need a lot of gas → the fill-up is expensive (y large).

Nearly full tank (x large): you need hardly any gas → the fill-up is cheap (y small).

x up, y down → negatively associated.

The reason this one catches people: gas and money feel like they belong together, so “positive” is the reflex. But the x here is the gas already in the tank, not the gas you buy. Read the variable definition, not the topic.

If x had been “gallons purchased”, the answer would be positive. One word changes the whole answer.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Positive would mean a fuller tank costs more to fill. It costs less — there is less room left to fill.
c.“Not associated” would mean the cost is unrelated to how much is already in the tank. It is very strongly related — in fact almost perfectly, since the tank has a fixed capacity.
🏷️ Try the two extreme cases in your head. Empty versus full settles the direction in seconds.

Question 4

📈 Reading a scatterplot

Estimating r from a scatterplot

Below is a plot of the Olympic gold-medal-winning performance in the high jump (in inches) for the years 1900 to 1996.

From this plot, the correlation between the winning height and year of the jump is
Olympic gold-medal high jump, 1900–1996
  • aabout 0.95.
  • babout 0.10.
  • cabout –0.50.
🔎 What it is really asking

Just by looking: is this relationship positive or negative, and how tight is it?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Reading r off a plot by eye. Two judgements, in this order: direction, then strength.

🛠️ The procedure

Direction from which way the cloud slopes. Strength from how tightly the points hug a straight line.

🧮 The working
Direction: the points march steadily upward from about 75 inches in 1900 to about 94 inches in 1996. Clearly positive, which kills option c immediately.

Strength: the points sit in a narrow rising band. You could lay a ruler through them and nothing would be far off it. That is a strong relationship — somewhere in the 0.9s.

So: about 0.95. (Computing r from the plotted points gives 0.968, so the eye was right.)

A rough scale worth carrying into the exam:
• a tight band, barely any scatter → 0.9 to 1.0
• a clear slope with visible spread → 0.6 to 0.8
• a vague tilt in a wide cloud → 0.3 to 0.5
• a shapeless blob → near 0
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.0.10 would be a shapeless cloud with almost no tilt. This plot is nothing like that — you can see the trend from across the room.
c.A negative r needs a downward cloud. This one rises.
🏷️ Direction first (it eliminates half the options), then strength from the tightness.

Question 5

🧮 Computing r and the line

Correlation from only two points

I wish to determine the correlation between the height (in inches) and weight (in pounds) of 21-year-old males. To do this I measure the height and weight of two 21-year-old men. The measured values are
Male #1Male #2
Height (in)7069
Weight (lb)160164
  • a1.0.
  • b–1.0.
  • cnear 0, because the heights and weights of the men are similar.
🔎 What it is really asking

What is r when you have exactly two data points?

🧭 Which type of question this is

A trick question with a clean rule behind it. The numbers barely matter; the count does.

🛠️ The procedure

Two points always lie exactly on a straight line, so |r| = 1 always. The only thing left to decide is the sign: does y go up or down as x goes up?

🧮 The working
The rule first. Any two points define a line exactly. There is no scatter around it, because there is nothing to scatter. So r is always +1 or −1 — never anything in between.

Now the sign.
Male #1: height 70, weight 160
Male #2: height 69, weight 164

Height goes down 70 → 69. Weight goes up 160 → 164. Opposite directions, so r = −1.0.

Confirm it the long way: SP = −2.0, SS(height) = 0.5, SS(weight) = 8.0, so r = −2.0 ⁄ √(0.5 × 8.0) = −1.0.

And the real lesson. A correlation of −1.0 from two men tells you nothing whatsoever about height and weight in the population. A perfect correlation on n = 2 is guaranteed by the arithmetic, not discovered in the data. You need a sample before r means anything.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.+1.0 would be right if weight had gone up with height. It went down.
c.This is the intuitive answer and it is impossible. “Similar values” cannot pull r towards 0 when there are only two points — r is ±1 no matter how close together they are. Closeness affects the slope, never the correlation.
🏷️ n = 2 → r = ±1, always. Work out only the sign, then stop.

Question 6

🥧 R² — how much is explained

From r to the percentage explained

A chemist was conducting an experiment to find how many mLs of a particular substance dissolve in different temperatures of water. A correlation of 0.87 was computed. Which interpretation is TRUE?
  • a87% of the variation in the amount of dissolved substance is explained by temperature.
  • bCorrelation can’t be computed because temperature is not a continuous variable.
  • c76% of the variation in the amount of dissolved substance is explained by temperature.
🔎 What it is really asking

r is 0.87. What percentage of the variation does that account for?

🧭 Which type of question this is

r versus r². The question is built entirely around the one step people skip.

🛠️ The procedure

“Percentage of the variation explained” is r², never r. Square it, then read it as a percentage.

🧮 The working
r = 0.87

r² = 0.87 × 0.87 = 0.7569 = 76%

So about 76% of the variation in how much dissolves is accounted for by temperature — and about 24% is down to everything else.

Why squaring matters so much. r² is always smaller than |r| (for any r between 0 and 1), and the gap grows as r falls:
• r = 0.9 → r² = 0.81 (81%)
• r = 0.7 → r² = 0.49 (49%) — a “strong” correlation explaining under half
• r = 0.5 → r² = 0.25 (25%) — a quarter
• r = 0.3 → r² = 0.09 (9%) — almost nothing

Reporting r as a percentage always overstates the case, which is exactly what option a does.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.87% is r read straight off as a percentage, with the squaring forgotten. This is the single most common error on the whole topic.
b.Temperature is perfectly continuous, and correlation is exactly the right tool for two quantitative variables. This option is testing whether you know when r applies at all.
🏷️ “Percentage explained” → square it first. r = 0.87 gives 76%, not 87%.

Question 7

🔮 Prediction and extrapolation

Prediction far outside the data

⚠ the arithmetic alone points elsewhere — read the note
Below is a plot of the Olympic gold-medal-winning performance in the high jump (in inches) for the years 1900 to 1996. The equation of the least-squares regression line of Winning Height (in inches) on Year is

Winning Height = –364.90 + 0.23 × Year

In another millennium (the year 3000), if the Olympics continue to be held, we can expect the Winning Height to be about:
Olympic gold-medal high jump, 1900–1996
  • a325 inches.
  • b690 inches.
  • cnone of the above.
🔎 What it is really asking

The line gives you a number for the year 3000. Should you believe it?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Extrapolation — one of your lecture’s four cautions. The arithmetic is a distraction; the judgement is the question.

🛠️ The procedure

Do the substitution, then ask the question that actually matters: is this x inside the range of the data? If it is far outside, the honest answer is that you cannot say.

🧮 The working
Step 1 — the arithmetic.
ŷ = −364.90 + 0.23 × 3000 = −364.90 + 690 = 325.1 inches

So the line does say about 325 inches, and option a is arithmetically correct.

Step 2 — the sanity check, which is the point of the question.
325 inches is 27 feet, or about 8.3 metres. The current world record is around 8 feet. Nobody is going to clear four and a half times their own height.

Step 3 — why the line breaks. The data run 1900 to 1996. The year 3000 is 1004 years past the last data point — ten times the length of the entire data set. Nothing in those twenty-two Olympics tells you anything about the year 3000. Records improve quickly at first and then flatten; the straight line has no way of knowing that.

So the answer is none of the above: we cannot expect any particular height.

⚠ Note the shape of this question. The arithmetic points at one letter and the correct answer is a different one. That is deliberate, and it is the most instructive question on the paper.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.325 inches is what the line says, and the line has been pushed a thousand years past its data. Correct arithmetic, worthless prediction — which is precisely the trap.
b.690 inches is the 0.23 × 3000 part with the intercept forgotten. Both wrong and absurd: 690 inches is 57 feet.
⚠ THE ARITHMETIC ALONE POINTS ELSEWHERE — READ THE NOTE

This is the one question on the paper where the arithmetic and the key disagree, and it is on purpose.

Substituting into the line gives 325.1 inches — exactly option a. The answer key is c, because a prediction 1004 years beyond the data is not a prediction at all. A straight line fitted to 1900–1996 carries no information about the year 3000.

If the x is far outside it, look for an option that says “none of the above”, “cannot be determined” or “the prediction is not reliable”. If one exists, it is almost certainly the answer; the arithmetic option was put there to catch you.

Your lecture’s own example makes the same point: Sarah’s height line predicts 88 cm at 42 months and 209.8 cm at 360 months. The first is useful, the second is nonsense, and the line cannot tell the difference. You have to.

📏 Reading slope and intercept

What a slope promises, and to whom

The regression line to predict average exam grade from hours of study is

ŷ = 15 + 5.6x

The slope of the regression line indicates
  • afor any student, an extra hour of study is predicted to increase the grade 5.6 points.
  • bon average, an extra hour of study is predicted to increase the grade 5.6 points.
  • can extra hour of study will increase the grade 15 points.
🔎 What it is really asking

The slope is 5.6. Whom is that promise about — every student, or students on average?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Slope interpretation, with the “on average” distinction as the whole point.

🛠️ The procedure

A slope is a statement about predicted averages: one more unit of x goes with b₁ more units of predicted y, on average. Never a guarantee for one individual.

🧮 The working
Slope = 5.6, so one extra hour of study goes with 5.6 more grade points in the prediction.

Why “on average” is not a hedge but the actual meaning. The line passes through the middle of a cloud of students. Real students sit above and below it — that is what the residuals are. For one particular student an extra hour might bring 12 points, or 1 point, or none. The 5.6 describes the average movement of the cloud, which is all a regression line ever describes.

This is also why option a is the single most common wrong answer in regression: the arithmetic is right and the subject of the sentence is wrong.

And the intercept, while you are here. b₀ = 15 is the predicted grade at x = 0 — a student who studied no hours at all. Here that is inside the plausible range, so it means something; often x = 0 is nowhere near the data and the intercept means nothing at all.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.“For any student” turns an average into a promise. The line cannot make promises about individuals — that is what the scatter around it is.
c.15 is the intercept, not the slope. It is the predicted grade for zero hours, not the effect of one more hour.
🏷️ Slope sentences need three things: the units, the words “on average”, and “predicted”.

Question 9

📈 Reading a scatterplot

Which conclusions a plot supports

Which of the following statements would be a valid conclusion from this plot of record times for men and women in some sport?
World record times. O = men, X = women
  • aThe world record times for women show a greater rate of improvement (decrease more rapidly) than the world record times for men.
  • bWe can expect the world record time for women to be lower than that for men sometime before the year 2010.
  • cBy the year 2010, the world record time for men will reach a plateau beyond which no improvement will be possible.
🔎 What it is really asking

Which statement describes only what the plot shows, without running either trend past the end of its data?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Reading a plot with two groups. One option describes; two extrapolate.

🛠️ The procedure

Sort the options into describing (what you can see) and predicting (what happens after the data stop). Only the describing one can be valid.

🧮 The working
What the plot shows. The women’s times (X) fall from about 12 800 to about 8 600 — a drop of roughly 4 200 over their span. The men’s times (O) fall from about 10 350 to about 7 600 — roughly 2 750, over a longer span. The women’s cloud is visibly steeper.

So a is a pure description of the picture. Valid.

Why b fails. It extends both lines past the data to find where they cross. That is extrapolation, and it assumes both trends stay straight — which record times never do. They flatten as they approach the limits of the human body.

Why c fails, and it fails twice. It extrapolates, and it then claims a plateau — something the plot cannot show at all. A straight-line fit, extended, never plateaus; and “no improvement will be possible” is a claim about human physiology, not about these points.

The pattern to carry away. On any plot question, an option containing a future date or the word “will” is almost always the wrong one. The safe option describes what is already drawn.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.This extends both trends beyond the data to find a crossing point. Even if the women’s line is steeper, nothing guarantees the trends continue — and record times flatten as they approach human limits.
c.Two errors in one sentence: it extrapolates, and then it claims a plateau the plot cannot show. A fitted straight line never plateaus, and “no improvement will be possible” is a claim about the human body, not about data.
🏷️ Options with a future date or the word “will” are extrapolation. The valid one describes what is drawn.

Question 10

🥧 R² — how much is explained

Which measure is pure strength

Which of the following measures only the strength of a relationship?
  • athe correlation coefficient (R)
  • bthe slope (b)
  • cthe Coefficient of Determination (R²)
🔎 What it is really asking

Three measures. Which one carries strength and nothing else?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Comparing what each number is actually made of. The word “only” is the whole question.

🛠️ The procedure

Go through each: does it carry direction? Does it carry units? Whatever is left carrying strength alone is the answer.

🧮 The working
R — carries strength and direction. −0.8 and +0.8 differ, and the difference is the direction. So not “only” strength.

b (the slope) — carries direction and units, and it is a rate of change, not a strength at all. A slope of 1000 can sit on a weak relationship (just change the units of y) and a slope of 0.001 on a near-perfect one. Slope says how much, not how tightly.

R² — squaring destroys the sign, so direction is gone, and it is a proportion, so units are gone. What is left is pure strength: the share of the variation in y explained. 0 to 1, nothing else attached.

So the answer is R².

The short version: R² is the only one of the three you cannot read a direction out of. That is exactly what makes it “only strength”.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.R is strength plus direction. The sign is doing real work in it — that was question 2.
b.The slope is a rate with units. Rescale y from metres to centimetres and the slope multiplies by 100 while the relationship is untouched.
🏷️ Squaring throws away the sign. That is what leaves R² carrying strength and nothing else.

🔎 What it is really asking

If r is negative, what do you know about the slope?

🧭 Which type of question this is

The link between r and b₁. One formula answers it completely.

🛠️ The procedure

Write down b₁ = r × s(y) ⁄ s(x) and look at what can be negative.

🧮 The working
The slope and the correlation are tied together by

b₁ = r × s(y) ⁄ s(x)

Now look at the pieces. s(y) and s(x) are standard deviations, so both are always positive. A positive number divided by a positive number is positive. So b₁ is r multiplied by something positive — which cannot change a sign.

c.They are very closely related — b₁ = r × s(y) ⁄ s(x) converts one into the other exactly.

  • c1200
  • d–1200
  • eCan’t be calculated

The car actually sold for $9000. How far is that from what the line predicted — and in which direction?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Residual = observed − predicted. Two steps, and the order of the subtraction is the whole question.

🛠️ The procedure

Residual = observed y − predicted ŷ. Predict first, then subtract in that order.

🧮 The working
Step 1 — predicted. From question 14, ŷ = $9600 for a five-year-old car.

Step 2 — observed. The actual selling price is $9000.

Step 3 — subtract, observed first.
residual = 9000 − 9600 = −600

🧭 Which type of question this is

Lurking variable. Both of the first two options are wrong, in opposite directions.

🛠️ The procedure

When an association looks odd, ask what third variable both things depend on. Then check whether the options name it.

🧮 The working
The third variable is staring at you: city size.

a.This is the causal over-claim. An observational association cannot establish cause, and the “men at the ballpark” story was constructed to fit the number.

Question 17

🔗 What r means

What averaging does to r

A survey of 1000 adults ages 30 to 35 is conducted. The number of years of schooling and the annual salary for each person in the survey is recorded. The correlation between years of schooling and annual salary is found to be 0.27. Suppose, instead, the average salary of all individuals in the survey with the same number of years of schooling was calculated and the correlation between these averages and years of schooling was computed. This correlation would most likely be
  • aequal to 0.27.
  • blarger than 0.27.
  • cless than 0.27.
🔎 What it is really asking

You replace 1000 individual salaries with one average salary per schooling level. Does r go up, down, or stay the same?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Aggregation. A pattern worth knowing as a fact, because the reasoning is slippery.

🛠️ The procedure

Ask what averaging removes. It removes the variation within each group and leaves only the pattern between groups — so the relationship looks tidier and r goes up.

🧮 The working
Before averaging. At “16 years of schooling” there are hundreds of people earning wildly different salaries — some 20 000, some 200 000. That person-to-person scatter is huge, and it is what holds r down to 0.27.

After averaging. Each schooling level becomes a single point: 12 years → one average, 13 years → one average, and so on. Perhaps fifteen points in total. All the within-group scatter has been averaged away. What is left is the clean upward trend of the averages.

Those few points lie much closer to a line, so r goes up, often dramatically — 0.27 can become 0.9 or more.

Why this matters beyond the exam. The underlying relationship has not changed one bit. Averaging has only hidden the noise. A paper that reports r = 0.95 from group averages and one that reports r = 0.27 from individuals can describe exactly the same data — so you must always ask what the unit of analysis was before you believe a correlation.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.It will not stay the same. The 1000 points have been collapsed into a handful, and the scatter that was holding r down has gone with them.
c.Less than 0.27 would mean averaging makes the relationship look messier. Averaging smooths; it does not add noise.
🏷️ Averaging removes within-group scatter and leaves the between-group trend, so r goes up — as a rule, and sharply.

A researcher notices that in a sample of adults, those who take greater amounts of vitamin C have fewer illnesses. However, those who take greater amounts of vitamin C also tend to exercise more.   • askewed

Two possible explanations travel together, so you cannot tell which is doing the work. What is that called?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Vocabulary, with a precise meaning behind it. The word is “confounded”.

🛠️ The procedure

Confounded means two explanatory variables are so tangled together in the data that their effects cannot be separated. Test it: can you find people with one but not the other?

🧮 The working
The situation. People who take more vitamin C also exercise more. So when you see fewer illnesses, you cannot tell whether it was:

• the vitamin C, or
• the exercise, or
• both, in some mixture.

The two explanations travel together, so their effects cannot be prised apart. That is exactly what confounded means.

The test to apply. Are there people in the data who take lots of vitamin C and do no exercise? And people who exercise hard and take no vitamin C? If those groups are missing, the two variables are confounded and no amount of clever analysis will separate them.

The fix. Randomly assign vitamin C, so that exercise levels end up balanced across the groups by chance. That breaks the link — which is what question 22 is about.

Confounded describes the relationship between two explanations: they travel together in these data, so their effects cannot be separated. The two words are not rivals — a lurking variable is usually confounded with the explanatory variable, which is exactly why it is a problem. The difference is what you are pointing at: one names a variable, the other names a tangle.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Skewness describes the shape of one variable’s distribution. It says nothing about two variables being tangled.
c.The response variable is the outcome — here, the number of illnesses. Vitamin C and exercise are both explanatory.
d.“Symmetric” describes a distribution’s shape, or the fact that r(x, y) = r(y, x). Neither is about two explanations being inseparable.
🏷️ Confounded = travelling together = effects cannot be separated. Ask whether anyone has one without the other.

How you actually establish cause

When possible, what is the best way to establish that an observed association is the result of a cause-and-effect relationship?
  • aStudy the least-squares regression line.
  • bObtain the correlation coefficient.
  • cUse a well-designed experiment.
  • dExamine z-scores rather than the original variables.
🔎 What it is really asking

Which of these four actually gets you from “associated” to “caused”?

🧭 Which type of question this is

The conclusion of the whole theme. Three options are statistics you compute; one is a study design.

🛠️ The procedure

Ask of each option: does it change what the data can support, or does it just describe the data differently? Only a change of design can do the former.

🧮 The working
Why none of the calculations can do it. The regression line, r, and z-scores are all ways of describing an association you already have. Your lecture makes the point sharply: causation, common response and confounding produce identical scatterplots. No statistic computed from those points can tell them apart, because the points are the same.

What an experiment changes. In a well-designed experiment you assign the explanatory variable at random rather than observing it. Random assignment spreads every lurking and confounding variable — measured or not, known or not — evenly across the groups by chance. If a difference then appears, the only thing left that differed systematically is the variable you assigned.

That is why it is the only route to cause, and why “well-designed” is in the option: random assignment, a control group, and enough participants.

The honest limit. Sometimes you cannot run the experiment — you cannot randomly assign people to smoke. Then you build a case from many studies, dose–response patterns and plausible mechanisms. That is a weaker argument, and the careful wording stays “associated with”.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.The regression line describes an association. It cannot tell you where the association came from.
b.Same problem. r measures how tight a relationship is, not why it exists.
d.z-scores just restate the same numbers on a standardised scale. In fact r is already built from z-scores — r = (1 ⁄ (n−1)) Σ zₓz_y — so this changes nothing at all.
🏷️ Statistics describe; design establishes. Random assignment is the only thing that breaks the link with lurking variables.

What establishes causation

A strong correlation has been found between two variables in an observational study. To establish that one causes the other, the researcher should:
  • arun a randomised experiment.
  • bcompute r² to see how much is explained.
  • ccollect a much larger sample.
  • dfit the regression line and check its slope.
🔎 What it is really asking

Which of these turns “associated” into “caused”?

🧭 Which type of question this is

The conclusion of the theme. Three options compute something; one changes the design.

🛠️ The procedure

Ask of each: does it change what the data can support, or just describe the same data differently?

🧮 The working
Why the calculations cannot help. r², the slope and the line all describe an association you already have. Direct causation, reverse causation and a common response produce identical scatterplots — no statistic computed from those points can tell them apart.

Why a bigger sample cannot help either. More data makes the estimate of the association more precise. A precisely measured confounded association is still confounded. Size fixes noise, not bias.

What an experiment changes. Random assignment spreads every lurking variable — measured or not, known or not — evenly across the groups by chance. If a difference then appears, the assigned variable is the only thing left that differed systematically.

That is the only route, which is why it is the answer.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.r² says how much variation is explained, not why. A confounded relationship can have a very high r².
c.A larger sample narrows the uncertainty around the same association. It cannot remove a lurking variable — it measures the confounded relationship more precisely.
d.The line and its slope describe the association. They cannot say where it came from.
🏷️ Statistics describe; design establishes. Only random assignment breaks the lurking-variable link.

If three situations produce the same scatterplot, then they produce the same r, the same line, the same r², the same residuals — everything you could compute, because all of it comes from those same points.

So no statistic can distinguish them. The information you need is not in the numbers.

Where it does come from:
• Design — was the explanatory variable assigned at random?
• Timing — did the supposed cause come first?
• Mechanism — is there a plausible way it could work?
• Subject knowledge — what else is known about these variables?

This is why “correlation is not causation” is not a caution to recite but a mathematical fact about what the data contain. And it is why the plot is still essential — it tells you about shape, outliers and groups, which no single number does. What it cannot tell you is why.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.The scatterplot is indispensable — for shape, outliers and hidden groups. It just cannot answer the causal question, which nothing in the data can.
b.Correlation says nothing about which explanation is right. All three stories can produce a correlation of 0.9.
d.r² is computed from the same points, so it is identical in all three cases. It cannot separate what the data do not separate.
🏷️ Identical plots give identical statistics. Causation is settled by design, never by computation.

Question 23

🧮 Computing r and the line

Slope from r and the standard deviations

A study reports r = 0.74, s(x) = 2.5 and s(y) = 8.0. The slope of the line predicting y from x is:
  • a2.37
  • b0.231
  • c0.74
  • d3.20
🔎 What it is really asking

Convert a correlation into a slope using the two standard deviations.

🧭 Which type of question this is

The r → b₁ conversion. The only risk is putting the SDs the wrong way up.

🛠️ The procedure

b₁ = r × s(y) ⁄ s(x). The y standard deviation goes on top, because the slope is in units of y per unit of x.

🧮 The working
b₁ = r × s(y) ⁄ s(x)

   = 0.74 × 8.0 ⁄ 2.5
   = 0.74 × 3.2
   = 2.368, which is 2.37 to two decimals — the form the options use.

How to remember which SD goes on top. The slope is “units of y per unit of x”, so y’s spread belongs on top, matching the units. If you ever forget, check the units of the answer: a slope must come out in y-units per x-unit.

The sign check: r is positive, so the slope is positive ✓ — they always match.

And the interpretation of the formula itself: a one-SD rise in x goes with an r-SD rise in y. That is your lecture’s “Fact 1”, and it is where the word “regression” comes from — since |r| < 1, y moves back towards its mean relative to x.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.0.231 has the standard deviations upside down. That would be the slope for predicting x from y.
c.0.74 is r itself. r and the slope coincide only when s(x) = s(y), which they do not here.
d.3.20 is s(y) ⁄ s(x) with r left out — the slope you would get if r were 1.0, which is its maximum possible value here.
🏷️ b₁ = r × s(y)/s(x). The y-SD goes on top, because the slope is in y-units per x-unit.

Using the screen-time data again (x̄ = 3, ȳ = 4, SP = −19, SS(x) = 10), the predicted hours of sleep for 4 hours of screen time, and that student’s residual, are:
Hours of screen time (x)12345
Hours slept (y)95321
  • a3.10 and −1.10
  • b2.10 and −0.10
  • c2.10 and 0.10
  • d4.00 and −1.00
🔎 What it is really asking

Build the line, predict at x = 4, then compare with what that student actually slept.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Slope, intercept, prediction and residual in one chain — exactly how an exam strings them together.

🛠️ The procedure

b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x) → b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄ → ŷ at x = 4 → residual = observed − predicted.

🧮 The working
Step 1 — slope.
b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x) = −19 ⁄ 10 = −1.90

Step 2 — intercept.
b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄ = 4 − (−1.90 × 3) = 4 + 5.70 = 9.70

So the line is ŷ = 9.70 − 1.90x.
<i>Check:</i> at x = x̄ = 3, ŷ = 4.00 = ȳ ✓

Step 3 — predict at x = 4.
ŷ = 9.70 − 1.90 × 4 = 2.10

Step 4 — residual. That student actually slept 2 hours.
residual = 2 − 2.10 = −0.10

Negative, so the student sits just below the line — they slept a fraction less than predicted. A residual this small means the point is almost exactly on the line.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Both numbers are out by exactly 1.00 — the prediction an hour too high, the residual an hour too low. c.The prediction is right and the residual has been subtracted the wrong way round (predicted − observed). Right size, wrong sign.
d.This is the prediction and residual for x = 3, not x = 4. One row off.

Question 25

🧮 Computing r and the line

Using the sum of residuals as a check

A student computes residuals of +2.1, −0.8, +1.4, −1.9 and −0.5 for five points. What does that tell you?
  • aThe fit is good, since most residuals are small.
  • bThe line is too steep, since the positives are larger.
  • cNothing — residuals need not sum to anything in particular.
  • dThere is an arithmetic error — they should sum to zero.
🔎 What it is really asking

Add the residuals up. What should the total be?

🧭 Which type of question this is

Using a property of least squares as an error check. One addition answers it.

🛠️ The procedure

For any least-squares line, the residuals sum to exactly zero. Add them and see.

🧮 The working
2.1 − 0.8 + 1.4 − 1.9 − 0.5 = 0.3

They do not sum to zero, so something is wrong — a mis-predicted value, a subtraction done the wrong way round, or an error in the line itself.

Why the sum must be zero. The least-squares line is positioned so that it passes through (x̄, ȳ). That is precisely the condition that makes the positive and negative residuals balance. It is a property of the method, not of the data — it holds for every least-squares fit, good or bad.

So it is a free check and a weak one. Free, because one addition catches arithmetic slips. Weak, because a sum of zero tells you nothing about whether the line fits — that is what the residual plot is for (question 9). A perfectly dreadful fit also has residuals summing to zero.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Most residuals being small says nothing here: the sum is wrong, so at least one of them has been computed incorrectly.
b.A steep or shallow line does not break the sum. Residuals sum to zero for every least-squares line, whatever its slope.
c.They must sum to exactly zero. That is a guaranteed property of the least-squares line, and it is the easiest check you will ever run.
🏷️ Residuals always sum to zero. Add them up — it is one addition and it catches real errors.
